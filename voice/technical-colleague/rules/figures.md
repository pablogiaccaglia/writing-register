# Figures

- {#figures.show-outputs} When a method produces images or other outputs, the text shows examples of those outputs beside the scores and says what they show, because a metric can count as a success what the pictures reveal as a failure.
- {#figures.requested-view} When the reader has asked for a particular kind of figure or view, the text shows that one and not a substitute.
- {#figures.representative-examples} A set of examples shows enough cases for the reader to judge what is typical: the worst and some randomly drawn cases beside the best, from each group the data is split into, chosen to cover the population rather than one convenient case.
- {#figures.beside-truth} A figure of an estimate shows, for every case and at every stage it draws, the true answer and the output of the standard reference method beside it, with the same panels for every method. When markers are drawn over an image, the image is also shown without them.
- {#figures.before-after} Where a method or a processing step transforms a signal, the text explains the step and a figure shows its input and output side by side.
- {#figures.didactic} A mathematical explanation is accompanied by figures that show what the formulas describe, so the reader is not left with a wall of symbols.
- {#figures.says-how-made} Each figure says what question it answers and how its images were made, naming the method and the inputs used; its caption describes exactly what is drawn, including which slice or projection is shown, what each axis, colour and marker encodes, and how to read it: what it shows when the method works.
- {#figures.consistent-panels} Panels meant to be compared are computed and drawn the same way, with the same overlays and markings, so a difference between them reflects the data and not the drawing.
- {#figures.labels} Every line, marker and panel in a figure carries a label that names what it shows and what distinguishes it, so no two panels share a label and the reader never guesses which curve is which.
- {#figures.layout} A figure answers one question and gets enough room to be read, with its text legible at the size shown. A figure that mixes several quantities, groups or processing stages is split into separate figures or into a grid of panels over several rows, each panel titled by what it shows; two views of one quantity may share a row.
- {#figures.beside-published} When a text compares its results with published work, it shows the published figures next to its own, reproduced as the same kind of plot and drawn in the same frame, so the reader can check the comparison.
- {#figures.inside-each-part} When a text describes a system of several parts, each part is drawn with its inputs, its internal steps and its output, in the same style as the others, and never as a single box.
- {#figures.field-conventions} A figure follows the conventions of published work in its field, for example a standard block diagram for a network drawn from the model itself, and prints numbers with enough digits, and summary values such as the range and the mean of an image, for the reader to tell compared values apart.
- {#figures.working-example} An example that illustrates how a method works is a case the method handles, chosen by a stated rule; failing cases are shown where failures are discussed, also chosen by a rule.
